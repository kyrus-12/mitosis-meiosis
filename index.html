General Overview
Believe it or not, you are made up of countless tiny units called cells. Every part of your body, including your skin, muscles, bones, and even your brain, is built from these cells, and together they make you who you are. It is almost as if you are a walking collection of cells, each working together to keep you alive. One of the most important processes cells perform is reproduction, how they create new cells so living things can grow, heal, and continue life. This process is part of the cell cycle, a repeating pattern in which cells grow, prepare, and then divide. Through cell reproduction, organisms can replace old or damaged cells and ensure that genetic information is passed on correctly to new cells. Without this process, living things could not grow, repair themselves, or survive. That is why cell reproduction is one of the most vital processes sustaining all plants, animals, and humans, keeping life going from one generation to the next.

CELL DIVISION AND KEY ORGANELLES
We'll start by defining cell division and identifying the key organelles you can observe during this process. Watch this video carefully, and take notes so you can better understand how cells grow, reproduce, and maintain life.


Key Ideas For You
• Cell division is not a standalone process. It is an integral part of the cell cycle, which goes through a series of stages as the cell grows, prepares, and finally divides into two new cells.

Key organelles in cell division include:
• Nucleus - the control center containing DNA.
• Centrioles - direct division by producing spindle fibers.
• Cell Membrane - the protective covering that regulates what enters and leaves the cell.

THE CELL CYCLE
Cell division is part of the longer process known as the cell cycle. In this video, we will explore what cell division is and how its phases work together to support cell growth and development.


Key Ideas For You

The cell cycle has 2 major phases: Interphase and Mitotic (M) Phase. Interphase prepares the cell for division and has 4 subdivisions:

• G1 Phase - Cell grows larger, absorbs nutrients, duplicates organelles, and performs normal functions.
• G0 Phase - Resting stage; the cell stops dividing, often due to DNA damage or other issues.
• S Phase - DNA is duplicated, and centrioles begin to replicate.
• G2 Phase - DNA replication is completed; the cell prepares for mitosis.

Regulatory proteins control the cycle, ensuring accuracy and healthy cell growth. Checkpoints act like quality control:


• G1 Checkpoint - Checks if the cell is big enough to divide.
• S Checkpoint - Ensures DNA is copied correctly.
• Mitosis Checkpoint - Makes sure chromosomes are aligned before division.

MITOSIS
After interphase, the cell enters the mitotic phase. What is this phase all about? What happens to the cell during this stage? Watch the video below to learn more.


Key Ideas For You
• Somatic cells (body cells like skin, muscle, bone, brain) contain 46 chromosomes (diploid, 2n).
• Diploid (2n) means two complete sets of chromosomes, one from mother and one from father.
• Humans inherit 23 chromosomes from each parent, forming the diploid set.
• Different organisms possess varying numbers of chromosomes. For example:

° Domestic cat (Felis catus): This species has a diploid number of 38 (2n= 38) in its somatic cells and a haploid number of 19 (n = 19) in its gametes.

° Domestic dog (Canis lupus familiaris): This species has a diploid number of 78 (2n = 78) in its somatic cells and a haploid number of 39 (n = 39) in its gametes.

° Philippine tarsier (Carlito syrichta): Endemic to the Philippines, this primate has a diploid number of 80 (2n = 80) in its somatic cells and a haploid number of 40 (n = 40) in its gametes.



Phases of Mitosis
• Interphase - Cell prepares for division; DNA replicates.
• Prophase - Nuclear membrane breaks down, chromosomes condense, centrioles move to poles.
• Metaphase - Chromosomes align at the cell’s center, spindle fibers attach to centromeres.
• Anaphase - Sister chromatids separate and move to opposite poles.
• Telophase - Nuclear envelope reforms; cleavage furrow (animal cells) or cell plate (plant cells) begins.
• Cytokinesis - Cytoplasm divides, forming two identical daughter cells with the same chromosome number.

MEIOSIS
After watching the video on mitosis, which replaces body cells, you might now be curious about the second type of cell division. Have you ever wondered why your family shares similar physical traits, such as hair color, skin tone, height, and more? This is where meiosis comes in.

Unlike mitosis, which focuses on growth and repair, meiosis produces reproductive cells and introduces genetic variation. Watch the video below to understand how meiosis creates the variety and uniqueness that make human beings different from one another.


Key Ideas For You
• Meiosis produces four genetically unique haploid gametes (sperm or egg cells) from a single diploid parent cell, halving the chromosome number from 46 to 23.
• Meiosis has two successive divisions, each with prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase, and cytokinesis.

° Prophase I: Chromosomes condense; homologous pairs form tetrads (synapsis). Crossing over occurs, creating recombinant chromosomes.

° Metaphase I: Tetrads align at the cell’s center.

° Anaphase I: Homologous chromosomes separate to opposite poles.

° Telophase I & Cytokinesis: Two haploid cells form.

° Meiosis II: This is similar to mitosis, where sister chromatids separate, resulting in four haploid gametes.

Gamete Production in Humans
• For Females (Oogenesis): Occurs in the ovaries. Egg cell development begins before birth, pauses, and resumes at puberty. Typically, one egg matures each month.
• For Males (Spermatogenesis): Occurs in the testes. Starts at puberty and continues daily. Each cell division produces four sperm cells, each with 23 chromosomes.


FUNCTIONS OF MITOSIS AND MEIOSIS
Mitosis and meiosis are the two main types of cell division that allow all living organisms, including plants and animals, to grow, reproduce, and continue life. These processes are essential for survival because they ensure that cells can multiply, repair, and pass on genetic information to the next generation.

Mitosis supports growth and repair. It produces two identical diploid cells, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent cell. The following are some of the functions of mitosis:
• Healing of wounds
• Replacing dead skin cells
• Growth of plants
• Increase in body height
• Formation of a zygote into an embryo

Meiosis supports sexual reproduction. It produces four genetically unique haploid cells, each with half the parent cell's number of chromosomes. This reduction prevents chromosome doubling in each generation and ensures genetic diversity. The following are some of the functions of meiosis:
• Formation of gametes
• Genetic variation – crossing over and independent assortment during meiosis create unique trait combinations.
• Continuation of species – by enabling sexual reproduction, meiosis ensures that plants and animals can produce offspring.

CELL CYCLE RELATED DISEASES
Aneuploidy occurs when a cell has an abnormal number of chromosomes (Cleveland Clinic, 2022). Normally, human cells have 46 chromosomes arranged in 23 pairs. But sometimes, during meiosis, chromosomes do not separate properly. This mistake, called nondisjunction, can cause a cell to have extra chromosomes or be missing some. When this cell becomes part of a baby, it may lead to certain conditions.

Examples of Aneuploidy
Figure 6
Down syndrome (Trisomy 21). Adapted from “Down’s Syndrome,” by BLK Pediatric Practice, 2022, https://blk-pediatric-practice.com/2022/11/13/downs-syndrome/. Copyright © BLK Pediatric Practice.


Figure 6 shows an image of a baby with Down syndrome. This occurs when an extra copy of chromosome 21 is present, giving them 47 chromosomes instead of the usual 46. Because of this extra chromosome, the body develops differently. People with Down syndrome may share certain physical features, such as a flatter face, smaller ears, and upward‑slanting eyes. They may also experience developmental delays in learning and growth. However, many individuals with Down syndrome live healthy lives and can go to school, work, and participate in their communities.

Figure 7
Image of cancer cells. Adapted from “Cancer,” by Cleveland Clinic, n.d., Cleveland Clinic, https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/12194-cancer. Copyright © Cleveland Clinic.


Another cell cycle–related disease is cancer, which occurs when cells lose control of their division and keep multiplying without stopping, as shown in Figure 7 above. This uncontrolled growth can form tumors and spread to other parts of the body. Conditions like Bloom syndrome and Fanconi anemia also result from problems with DNA repair during the cell cycle. These diseases show how important it is for the cell cycle to work correctly, because even small mistakes can affect growth, development, or health (Rayi & Hozayen, 2022).
